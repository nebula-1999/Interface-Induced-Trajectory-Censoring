Mistral is the one family whose failure announces itself (HTTP 400 on a repeated
\texttt{{[}TOOL\_CALLS{]}} marker). The other three --- and a missing \texttt{-\/-enable-auto-tool-choice} aside ---
return HTTP 200 with \texttt{tool\_calls:\ {[}{]}}, which is byte-identical to a model that declined to

dominate and are the hardest to attribute.}

\textbf{A false-positive capability check.} The natural pre-flight test --- does
\texttt{apply\_chat\_template(tools=\ldots{})} render the schema into the prompt? --- correctly rejects
DeepSeek-Coder but \textbf{passes Qwen2.5-Coder}, whose \texttt{tokenizer\_config.json} inherits Hermes
scaffolding from Qwen2.5-Instruct while the checkpoint was never trained on it.
\textbf{Protocol support is a per-checkpoint property and is not inferable from the chat
template}: templates are inherited, training is not.

\subsection{A 23\% failure that survived three controls and fell to the fourth}
\label{sec:llama}

\textbf{the function the task asks it to implement} --- \texttt{can\_form\_word}, \texttt{check\_password\_strength},

\textbf{Where the residual gap lives.} With \texttt{strict} enabled, FC and ReAct have \emph{identical}
execution mechanics --- 98 calls, 0 wrong-tool, 97 executed on both sides --- yet turn-1 pass
differs 46 vs 61. Once the interface is equalised, \textbf{what remains is first-draft code
quality under the protocol, not tool-use mechanics.}

\emph{(One \texttt{strict} call is issued and syntactically valid but yields no test result: item 53
imports \texttt{seaborn}, unavailable in the sandbox, so the harness collected zero tests. It is
counted as a failure, not silently dropped.)}

The rich schema explicitly warned \emph{``this tool is not the function you are asked to
implement; do not pass the task function's parameters''} and supplied an example --- the
confusion persisted at 22/100. The CoT control is the more interesting negative: asking the
model to reason about the task's signature and edge cases \textbf{before} constructing the call
raised confusion from 23 to 59, because the parameters it had just reasoned about were the
ones it then supplied. \textbf{On an interface with an ambiguous role boundary, more reasoning
amplifies misuse rather than correcting it.}

The defensible statement is narrower than either ``model defect'' or ``configuration bug'':
\textbf{the model exhibits role confusion under unconstrained function calling, and the interface
constraint determines whether that confusion can be expressed as an environment action.}
We cannot show from these experiments that the underlying tendency persists once
constrained --- only that it stops reaching the action space. vLLM's \texttt{auto} mode is
unconstrained by default.

\textbf{This section is itself an instance of the paper's claim.} We approached with explicit
suspicion, ran three single-variable paired controls, and still wrote a configuration
problem into a draft as a model deficiency.

\textbf{Internal validity check.} The two FC arms have \textbf{identical turn-1 pass (53 vs 53)}. The

\textbf{Why Llama's turn-1 does move (34$\to$46) while Qwen's does not.} This is not an
inconsistency; it is the taxonomy making a prediction. Qwen's failure sits at the \textbf{parser
layer}, downstream of generation: the first-turn completion is produced and then
discarded, so repairing the parser cannot change it --- hence 53 vs 53. Llama's failure sits
at the \textbf{schema layer}, inside generation: the model spends its entire first turn calling
the wrong function, so the first-turn output is itself destroyed and repairing the
constraint restores it --- hence 34 $\to$ 46. \textbf{Different failure layers leave different
downstream signatures, and the direction of the turn-1 effect identifies the layer.}

The pass-rate gain is smaller and \textbf{not significant} (53$\to$62, \emph{p}=0.093).

Instrumentation is placed at the two agent-side call sites (\texttt{CodeTool.execute},
\texttt{ReActAgentLoop.\_run\_tests}). It \textbf{must not} be placed in the sandbox entry point: the
reward function evaluates final code through the same function, which would merge reward

lines, all tagged \texttt{react}.

Three independent signals agree: \texttt{num\_turns} pinned at its minimum of 2, tool time exactly
0.00 s, dedicated count 0.

\textbf{Wording bound.} What is measured is \textbf{parser-accepted and executed calls = 0}, \emph{not}
``the model never attempted to call''. FC rollouts were not saved, so direct answers,
malformed calls and dropped intent cannot be separated from the training logs. A probe
replication under the identical mandatory prompt on the same 1.5B checkpoint (n=100) gives:
\textbf{0 well-formed calls, 54 direct answers, 46 unparsable outputs, 66 items containing JSON
fragments but not one naming \texttt{run\_tests}.} Notably the mandatory instruction \emph{degraded}
the model --- final pass 31 $\to$ 15 versus the optional prompt --- leaving it able neither to call
the tool nor to answer cleanly.

\textbf{Why gradient cannot fix this.} Valid calls are zero \textbf{from step 1}, and the reward
admits direct answers (FC \texttt{critic/rewards/mean} rose 0.233$\to$0.281). The policy gradient
therefore contains \textbf{no signal pushing toward tool use}: the tool branch has a
zero-sample return, while direct answering is continuously reinforced. The model cannot
discover the tool path by exploration, because every attempt is discarded at the protocol
layer and no positively-rewarded tool trajectory ever exists to be reinforced.
\textbf{A mandatory prompt plus GRPO does not cross this cold start.}

\begin{quote}
Interface-induced censoring does not merely weaken a learning signal.
The tool-using branch receives \textbf{no direct on-policy gradient signal under the
observed rollout distribution}, and is \textbf{effectively inaccessible to policy-gradient
learning in this cold-start regime}.
\end{quote}

\textbf{Causal chain.}

{\footnotesize
\begin{verbatim}
Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B emits nothing hermes can parse
  (probe replication under the training prompt: 0 parsed, 0 tight, 66 JSON fragments)
    -> parser-accepted calls in training rollouts = 0 (num_turns pinned at 2)
      -> Observation never returns; trajectories degenerate to single-turn
        -> RL optimises only a single-turn signal
          -> multi-turn rescues flat at 6-9/540;
           91-94% of new passes are turn-1
\end{verbatim}
}

The last two links are the training result of \S1; the middle two are measured here.

\subsubsection{Why no repaired-FC training arm exists}

The natural control is \emph{repaired} FC rather than ReAct. We attempted to construct it and
failed at three successive layers; each attempt is a measurement, so we report them.

\textbf{(i) A vLLM parser plugin does not reach the training loop.} verl's AgentLoop performs its
own tool-call parsing (\texttt{verl/experimental/agent\_loop/tool\_parser.py}, registered as
\texttt{hermes}, adapted from vLLM v0.9.1). \texttt{multi\_turn.format=hermes} selects \emph{that} parser, not
the server's. Attaching a plugin to the vLLM rollout server therefore changes nothing in
training --- a distinction worth stating because the two look identical from the outside.

\textbf{(ii) A verl-side parser recovers calls, but none of them are the tool.} We implemented a
implemented a \texttt{qwen2\_5\_coder} parser inside verl's registry accepting
\texttt{<tools>} tags, \texttt{json} fenced blocks and bare JSON. On the exact training
condition (1.5B, mandatory prompt, $n{=}100$) it lifts recoverable calls from
\textbf{0/100 (hermes) to 52/100}. Of those 52, \textbf{zero name} \texttt{run\_tests}.

\textbf{(iv) Constrained decoding is unavailable in this stack.} The remedy that works at
evaluation time --- forcing the tool choice, or schema-constrained decoding --- is not exposed
by verl 0.9.0's vLLM rollout path (its only \texttt{strict} key belongs to the profiler).

We therefore state the limitation precisely: \textbf{no executable repaired-FC condition was
available through the existing verl 0.9.0 vLLM rollout configuration.} This is a property
of the training stack, not a proof that FC is irreparable in principle; forced tool choice
via a rollout backend that exposes guided decoding remains untested. Accordingly, \textbf{ReAct
serves as a positive-control interaction channel, not as a parser-repair control}, and the
comparison changes protocol and interface together. We flag this rather than let the
comparison be read as a clean single-variable intervention.

\textbf{Scope.} This holds for Qwen2.5-Coder + \texttt{hermes}. It does not generalise to ``FC training
never receives tool feedback''. Whether the \S5.5 adapter also restores the training signal
was not tested. The demonstration is 10 and 3 steps --- a \textbf{mechanism} result, not an
outcome comparison.

\subsubsection{Opening the channel does not, by itself, unlock multi-turn learning}
\label{sec:sufficiency}

The censoring result above says a broken interface removes the tool-using branch from the
experience distribution. It does \emph{not} say that restoring the channel is \emph{sufficient}
for the policy to learn multi-turn repair. We tested that directly.

\textbf{What this arm is, and what it is not.} Since no executable repaired-FC condition exists
in this stack (previous subsection), we ran the one configuration that does deliver tool
feedback at this scale: \textbf{ReAct}, 75 GRPO steps, same model, data, hyper-parameters,
seed and evaluation hook as the three historical runs. The run executed \textbf{23{,}676 tool
calls}. \textbf{This is a ReAct arm, not a repaired-FC arm}: it changes protocol and
interface together, so it is a test of whether \emph{any} working tool channel suffices, not
an isolation of the parser. We state this explicitly because the weaker reading --- that a
parser repair was applied to the FC arm --- is not what was run and is not what we claim.

\textbf{What this does and does not license.} It does not license ``interface repair unlocks

It licenses the narrower statement we defend: \textbf{the interface determines whether
tool-mediated trajectories exist at all, and their absence is sufficient to explain why no

multi-turn learning at this scale and budget.} The bottleneck is not singular.

the comparison covers the first 60 evaluated steps of each. The step-75 evaluation was lost:

missing point would not change the reading, but we note the loss rather than presenting an
unexplained gap. Because this arm is not a parser-repair control, it cannot separate
``a working channel is insufficient'' from ``ReAct in particular is insufficient''; the
experiment that would separate them is named in \S7.

\subsection{Pressure on a broken interface makes things worse}

Two independent controls point the same way. Adding a reasoning scaffold to FC raised
role confusion from 23 to \textbf{59} (\S5.3): asked to analyse the task function's signature
before acting, the model supplied exactly those parameters to the tool. Replacing the
optional tool instruction with a mandatory one (\emph{``you must call \texttt{run\_tests}; do not answer
directly''}) on Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B moved parser-accepted calls not at all --- still 0/100 ---
while final pass fell from \textbf{31 to 15} and unparsable outputs rose from \textasciitilde0 to 46/100.

Neither intervention touches the interface, and both make the observable outcome worse.
Coercion cannot substitute for a working channel: the model is pushed away from the
answer it could give and toward a call that will be discarded anyway.

\textbf{Practical reading.} When an agent appears reluctant to use tools, strengthening the
instruction is the cheapest thing to try and the most likely to mislead --- it can degrade
task performance while leaving the tool-call count at zero, which looks like a model that
is both unwilling \emph{and} incapable.

\subsection{Multi-turn repair stabilises the total while its composition keeps moving}
\label{sec:composition}

Two independent observations, from different axes, show the same structure.

\textbf{Across sampling seeds} (\S5.8): ReAct's final pass is 72 / 72 / 72 at \texttt{temperature=0.6},
yet the sets of solved items have pairwise Jaccard 0.71--0.80 --- 15--24 items differ between
seeds. Turn-1 pass varies widely (43--56, sd 6.8) and the rescue count compensates inversely
(19 / 16 / 29).

\textbf{Across training steps} (\S\ref{sec:sufficiency}): between step 15 and step 30 of the ReAct run, 85 of 542
first-turn completions changed verbatim and 6 items flipped outcome --- 3 gained, 3 lost ---
leaving turn-1 and final pass numerically identical (340 / 346 at both checkpoints). The
policy is demonstrably moving; the aggregate is not.

The common structure is that \textbf{the repair loop absorbs perturbation upstream of it}.
Whatever the first draft does --- vary with sampling, drift with training --- items that fail
and are repairable get repaired, and the total lands in the same place. This has two
consequences worth separating:

\emph{For measurement.} An aggregate pass rate is a \textbf{poor instrument for detecting change in a
multi-turn agent}: it is stabilised by the very mechanism under study. Item-level set
comparison (Jaccard, flip counts) detects movement that the total conceals. We report both
throughout, and note that had we reported only totals we would have concluded --- twice, on
two different axes --- that nothing was happening.

\emph{For deployment.} Stability of the total under sampling noise is a property practitioners
actually want, and it is contributed by the multi-turn loop rather than by the base policy.
It is invisible in single-turn evaluation.

\textbf{Bound.} Both observations are on one model family and one task. The identical totals are
in part coincidence --- three seeds landing on exactly 72, two checkpoints landing on exactly
340/346 --- and we do not claim the aggregate is invariant, only that it is far less sensitive
than its components.
