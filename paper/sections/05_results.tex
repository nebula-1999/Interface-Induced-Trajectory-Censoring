\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The same mismatch on a standard benchmark, and a $2\times2$ that localises it}
\label{sec:bfcl}

We begin on someone else's instrument. BFCL~v4 supplies official data, a multi-turn executor and a
scorer at pinned Gorilla commit \texttt{6ea57973c7a6097fd7c5915698c54c17c5b1b6c8}. Both arms serve
Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct on vLLM~0.27.1 at \texttt{temperature = 0}, seed 0, on 100
\texttt{simple\_python} and 100 \texttt{multi\_turn\_base} cases identical in both arms
(Appendix~\ref{app:bfcl}); weights, cases, decoding, seeds, executor and scorer are fixed and
\emph{only the serving adapter configuration} changes. We say \emph{adapter configuration}, not
\emph{parser}: the repaired arm substitutes the community parser together with its paired chat
template, so the intervention is the pair, and we ran the $2\times2$ that separates them.

\small

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}

\midrule
\texttt{hermes} parser & 0/200 & \textbf{0/200} \\
dedicated parser & \textbf{0/200} & 196/200 \\

BFCL's own scorer gives 0.00 for the three zero cells and 0.96 / 0.19 for the fourth.
\textbf{The effect cannot be apportioned because it is not additive: both main effects are exactly
zero and all of it sits in the interaction.} Under the documented template the model writes a bare
call inside a \texttt{```json} fence; under the dedicated template it writes the same payload,
byte for byte, inside \texttt{<tools>} tags. Each parser behaves correctly for the envelope it is
defined to accept, so no component is defective and repairing one side of the contract buys
exactly nothing.

Under the configuration a practitioner reaches by following the documentation the server parses
zero of 200 cases while 166 of those same first responses contain a call meeting the strict
criterion. The consequence propagates to every downstream layer, because BFCL executes the calls
it parses and scores the resulting state: on \texttt{multi\_turn\_base} the repaired arm parses
97 first calls, executes 98 and succeeds on 19, against 0/0/0 for \texttt{hermes}
(Table~\ref{tab:bfcl5}), whose success count is therefore zero \emph{by construction rather than
by capability}. \textbf{A published benchmark
number for this model can differ by the entire span of the metric depending on a serving flag the
model card does not mention.} We make no claim that 0.19 is a good score --- only that 0.00 is not
a measurement of the model. The repaired arm uses the community adapter, not a verified-optimal
one, and 4 of its 200 first requests still fail to parse, so $0\to19$ is a \textbf{lower bound} on
what the interface was removing.

One deviation from stock BFCL is required and we state it plainly: its shipped handler for local
Qwen models posts to \texttt{/v1/completions} and extracts \texttt{<tool\_call>} blocks with a
regular expression \emph{inside the benchmark process}, so the serving parser is never invoked and
the quantity under study cannot appear. We instead route BFCL's own OpenAI handler through
\texttt{/v1/chat/completions}, adding only \texttt{tool\_choice: auto} and trace headers; dataset
loading, execution and scoring remain BFCL's code, and \texttt{bfcl\_eval}'s files are left
byte-identical. Running the pair twice reproduces the \texttt{hermes} arm exactly and moves the
repaired arm by one case with success 19 in both, so we report that arm's intermediate columns as
$\pm1$ (Appendix~\ref{app:bfcl}).

\paragraph{A second external benchmark, interactive and multi-turn.}
BFCL scores a single trajectory against a final state. $\tau$-bench \citep{yao2025taubench} adds a
simulated user and a stateful environment, so a censored call costs not one action but the rest of
the conversation. We ran its 115 \texttt{retail} tasks under both arms, holding weights, tasks,
seeds, user simulator and concurrency fixed and changing only the serving adapter.

The mechanism transfers intact: every one of 1389 requests carried \texttt{tools}, not one was
parsed, and 789 assistant turns carried a call-shaped payload that the server discarded. Under the
repaired adapter 103 of 115 tasks reach a tool execution, against \textbf{zero} before.

the documented arm's solved set is a strict subset of the repaired arm's. Two scope conditions
belong with these numbers rather than in a footnote. The user simulator is a locally served

scores are not comparable to the $\tau$-bench leaderboard} and this experiment claims only the
between-arm difference; and the arms differ in conversation length by construction, which is why
every quantity above is reported per task and not per request.

\subsection{Censoring against scale, and the same scale under a matched interface}
\label{sec:scale}

The same funnel on our own probe, across the Qwen2.5-Coder ladder under \texttt{tool\_choice:
auto} and \texttt{hermes} --- the documented recommendation for Qwen2.5. At 32B,
\texttt{strong = 100} and \texttt{tight = 80} means every item contained a named call and 80
carried a usable payload.

Across a 21$\times$ range the server reports a flat zero while well-formed emitted calls rise to
80/100. \textbf{Within this family, the larger the checkpoint, the larger the absolute undercount
under the same mismatched interface.} This is a within-family observation across five
checkpoints, not a law relating capability to censoring: parameter count is not capability, and
we have one family's ladder. Raw-tag counts reproduce vLLM issue \#32926's baseline exactly:
across all sizes \texttt{<tool\_call>} and \texttt{<tools>} appear zero times.

Two supporting analyses are in Appendix~\ref{app:reparse}. Re-parsing the \emph{same stored
bytes} under four rules excludes ``your extractor is simply better than \texttt{hermes}'': the
\texttt{hermes} column is zero at every scale because the model never produces that format, while
the bare-JSON and tight columns are not. Replaying vLLM's extractor line for line to separate the
layer at which each item is lost shows that \textbf{genuine parser loss is zero in every arm}, and
across all 4254 de-duplicated first turns in the archive; the single place where parser strictness
alone costs anything is Instruct-3B, where 26 payloads are valid JSON followed by trailing text
that a lenient \texttt{raw\_decode} would recover.\footnote{The 1.5B row uses the \emph{optional}
tool instruction. Under the \emph{mandatory} prompt used for training (\S\ref{sec:rl}) the same
checkpoint gives weak = 66 and unparsable = 46: coercion produces more JSON-shaped debris, not
more valid calls. The two numbers describe different prompts, not a discrepancy.}

\paragraph{A counterfactual ladder: scale under a \emph{matched} interface.}
The server column is flat zero, so the growth above comes entirely from the emitted column ---
and larger checkpoints emit more well-formed JSON for reasons unrelated to tool calling. The
clean test is a ladder under an interface that \emph{matches}: if the undercount is a property of
scale it should grow there too. Qwen3 supplies one --- its template injects tools and requires
\texttt{<tool\_call>}, the envelope \texttt{hermes} accepts, and it ships a dense size ladder. We
committed the prediction (``parsed $>0$ at every size'') to the repository before running
(\texttt{p4/PREREGISTRATION.md}).

\textbf{Across a comparable span of scale under a matched envelope, the silent fraction does not

envelope, since the ladder changes family and envelope together. Two further caveats we state
rather than bury: the parsed column is \textbf{not monotone} (86, 44, 70, 97), so this ladder
shows presence, not a trend; and Qwen3 was served with \texttt{enable\_thinking=false}, because
with thinking on the model spends its entire token budget in a \texttt{<think>} block and is
truncated before it emits a call --- a confound unrelated to the envelope, recorded as a protocol
deviation before the run.

A second family exhibiting the \emph{same} undercount would be stronger still, and we did not get
one. Of nine families surveyed beforehand, six do not inject OpenAI-style tools at all --- they
cannot exhibit envelope-layer censoring, though template omission is itself a failure layer ---
and of the remaining three, Granite-3.1-8B turned out not to attempt tool calls on this task at
all. Under \texttt{hermes} and under its own \texttt{granite} parser both give 0, and
\textbf{the rescue arm is what tells us the arm is uninformative rather than confirmatory}. We
claim nothing from it.

\subsection{Within-lineage control: the same ladder in Qwen2.5-Instruct}
\label{sec:instruct}

lineage is mismatched. Changing family cannot settle it: Llama parses correctly, Mistral returns

informative control is inside the lineage. Qwen2.5-\textbf{Instruct} shares one chat template
with Coder --- Coder inherits it, which is what produces the false-positive template check of

on the same items and criterion, shows it is not. We therefore state the repair result as a
ceiling rather than an absence: \textbf{how much multi-turn recovers is bounded by how much of the
tool channel the interface leaves open.}

\subsection{A 23\% failure that survived three controls and fell to the fourth}
\label{sec:llama}

Llama-3.1-8B under FC issues a tool call on 97/100 items, but on 23 of them the call names
\emph{the function the task asks it to implement} --- \texttt{can\_form\_word},
\texttt{check\_password\_strength}, \texttt{floyd\_warshall} --- with that function's own
parameters as arguments. A re-run restricted to those 23 items under matched configuration
reproduced 23/23. \emph{This also corrects a measurement of our own}: because the probe did not
originally verify the tool name, these 23 were counted as initiations, so \textbf{Llama's true
\texttt{run\_tests} initiation rate is 74/100, not 97/100} (Appendix~\ref{app:errata}).

Four single-variable paired controls (tabulated in Appendix~\ref{app:extra}). A richer schema
that explicitly warned \emph{``this tool is not the function you are asked to implement''} left
the confusion at 22/100 (\emph{p}=1.000). A ReAct-style Thought scaffold before the call raised it
from 23 to 59 (\emph{p}\textless0.001, \emph{worse}): asked to reason about the task function's
signature first, the model then supplied exactly those parameters. Adding the official chat
template moved nothing (23 $\to$ 22, \emph{p}=0.125). Enabling \texttt{strict: true} took it to 0
(\emph{p}=0.0001). The first three rejected their alternative explanations and pointed at the
model; the fourth found the cause and withdrew that conclusion.

Roughly \textbf{two fifths of what looked like a protocol effect was an interface effect}, and

quality under the protocol, not tool-use mechanics. The defensible statement is narrower than
either ``model defect'' or ``configuration bug'': \textbf{the model exhibits role confusion under
unconstrained function calling, and the interface constraint determines whether that confusion
can be expressed as an environment action.} We cannot show that the tendency disappears once
constrained, only that it stops reaching the action space. vLLM's \texttt{auto} mode is
unconstrained by default.\footnote{One \texttt{strict} call is syntactically valid but yields no
test result: item 53 imports \texttt{seaborn}, unavailable in the sandbox, so zero tests were
collected. It is counted as a failure, not silently dropped.}

\subsection{Four layers, and what a pre-flight check can see}
\label{sec:taxonomy}
\label{sec:families}

The two families above fail at different layers and admit different remedies: Qwen2.5-Coder at the
\emph{parser} layer, repaired by a dedicated adapter, and Llama-3.1-8B at the \emph{schema}
layer, repaired by \texttt{strict: true}. Neither is detectable in advance by the obvious check
(Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}). Two further families extend the taxonomy without supporting a quantitative comparison, and we
report them as such. \textbf{DeepSeek-Coder} fails at the \emph{template} layer --- its chat
template never injects tools, which a template check correctly catches, and we found no remedy.
\textbf{Mistral-7B-v0.3} fails at the \emph{token} layer, repeating a \texttt{[TOOL\_CALLS]}
marker that produces HTTP 400, and has no admissible 100-item FC run at all: all four
configurations produce request errors (parser only 2; official template 42; parser + strict 3;
official template + strict 39), so vLLM's own recommended configuration \emph{raises} the error
rate from 2\% to 42\% and \texttt{strict}, which fixes Llama, does not help. The correct statement
is not ``the two protocols do not differ on Mistral'' but that no such comparison exists.

Mistral is the one family whose failure announces itself; the other three return HTTP 200 with
\texttt{tool\_calls: []}, byte-identical to a model that declined to call the tool. \textbf{The
claim is not that all failures are silent; it is that the silent ones dominate and are the
hardest to attribute.} The natural pre-flight test --- does \texttt{apply\_chat\_template(tools=...)}
render the schema? --- correctly rejects DeepSeek-Coder but \emph{passes} Qwen2.5-Coder, whose
\texttt{tokenizer\_config.json} inherits Hermes scaffolding from Qwen2.5-Instruct while the
checkpoint was never trained on it: protocol support is a per-checkpoint property and is not
inferable from the chat template.

\subsection{Repair loop: how much comes back}
\label{sec:repair}
\label{sec:maintable}

\begin{center}
\small
\captionof{table}{Repair loop on Qwen2.5-Coder-7B, \texttt{tool\_choice: auto} fixed, changing only the adapter.}\label{tab:repair}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lllllll@{}}
\toprule
Arm & Parsed & Mean turns & $\geq$2 turns & Turn-1 & Final & Rescued \\
\midrule
ReAct & 95 & 1.92 & 40 & 57 & \textbf{74} & 17 \\
FC + hermes & \textbf{0} & 0.96 & \textbf{0} & 53 & 53 & \textbf{0} \\
FC + dedicated adapter & \textbf{84} & 1.82 & \textbf{37} & 53 & \textbf{62} & \textbf{9} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Parse 0$\to$84, items reaching a second turn 0$\to$37, multi-turn rescues 0$\to$9: \textbf{the
mechanism is restored from literal zero}. The two FC arms have identical turn-1 pass (53 vs 53),
which is the internal-validity check --- an adapter can only affect what happens \emph{after} the
first turn. That Llama's turn-1 \emph{does} move (34$\to$46) while Qwen's does not is the taxonomy
making a prediction: Qwen's failure sits at the parser layer, downstream of generation, so the
first-turn completion is produced and then discarded; Llama's sits at the schema layer, inside
generation, so the first-turn output is itself destroyed and repairing the constraint restores it.
Different failure layers leave different downstream signatures, and the direction of the turn-1
effect identifies the layer.

The pass-rate gain is \emph{not} significant (53$\to$62, \emph{p}=0.093). A pre-planned
replication on a random 300-item sample reproduces the direction with three times the data
(160/300 $\to$ 178/300, 43 discordant against 25, \emph{p}=0.0385), nominally below 0.05 --- but
this paper runs roughly a dozen McNemar tests and the Bonferroni threshold at that family size is
$\approx$0.0042. \textbf{The repair gain therefore still does not survive correction, and we rest
no claim on it.} What the larger sample establishes is that the direction is stable and not an
artefact of the non-random subset. The protocol gap on the same 300 items (178/300 $\to$ 219/300,
\emph{p}=3.1$\times$10$^{-6}$) clears the corrected threshold by three orders of magnitude.

Two bounds. Restricting to items both arms parsed, the residual protocol gap holds on Llama
(\emph{p}=0.0021) and is not detected on Qwen: $+8.4$\,pp, 95\% CI $[-0.5, +17.4]$,
\emph{p}=0.118. \textbf{We do not read $p>0.05$ as evidence of no effect} --- that interval admits
a residual larger than the effect we detect on Llama --- so the residual protocol effect is
established on one family and undetermined on the other (Appendix~\ref{app:conditioned}). And the
dedicated solution changes parser, chat template and few-shot simultaneously, so at this
granularity it can only be called an \emph{adapter combination}; the $2\times2$ of
\S\ref{sec:bfcl} is what separates the components.

\label{sec:rl}

mandatory instruction (\emph{``you must call \texttt{run\_tests} to verify your code; do not
answer directly''}).

\textbf{Wording bound.} What is measured is \textbf{parser-accepted and executed calls = 0},
\emph{not} ``the model never attempted to call'': FC rollouts were not saved, so direct answers,
malformed calls and dropped intent cannot be separated from the training logs.

\paragraph{At 7B, the training stack censors a call that is there.}
The 1.5B checkpoint's zero is over-determined. A probe replication under the identical mandatory
prompt (n=100) gives \textbf{0 well-formed calls, 54 direct answers, 46 unparsable outputs, and 66
items containing JSON fragments but not one naming \texttt{run\_tests}} --- at that scale the
missing calls are a capability failure, not a parser one, and there was nothing well-formed to
censor. (The mandatory instruction also \emph{degraded} the model: final pass 31 $\to$ 15 versus
the optional prompt.) That leaves the question the RL claim needs: inside the training rollout
path, does a model that \emph{does} emit well-formed calls have them removed? We ran the probe of
\S\ref{sec:scale} a second time, not against a serving endpoint but inside verl's own AgentLoop,
on Qwen2.5-Coder-7B under the documented configuration, logging every generation, the parser's
verdict, tool executions and observations. One rollout batch suffices; no training step is
required.

\textbf{Forty-five of 115 generations carry a call that would execute if it were wrapped in the
envelope, and none reaches the environment.} The mechanism is the one \S\ref{sec:scale} identifies
on the serving side, now observed \emph{inside the training loop}, which is where the RL claim
needs it: at 7B the absence of tool-mediated trajectories is attributable to the interface, not to
the policy. It does not retroactively change the 1.5B run, whose zero remains over-determined; we
report the two scales separately and the scale dependence is itself the finding. This probe's run
terminated with a \textbf{CUDA OOM} during the actor update that follows rollout collection, so no
gradient step completed; the rollout observations were all produced before that point and are
unaffected.

Valid calls are zero from step 1, and the reward admits direct answers (FC
\texttt{critic/rewards/mean} rose 0.233$\to$0.281), so the policy gradient contains no signal
pushing toward tool use: the tool branch has a zero-sample return while direct answering is
continuously reinforced. The model cannot discover the tool path by exploration, because every
attempt is discarded at the protocol layer and no positively-rewarded tool trajectory ever exists
to be reinforced. \textbf{The tool-using branch receives no direct on-policy gradient signal under
the observed rollout distribution, and is effectively inaccessible to policy-gradient learning in
this cold-start regime.} This holds for Qwen2.5-Coder + \texttt{hermes} and does not generalise to
``FC training never receives tool feedback''; at 10 and 3 steps it is a \emph{mechanism} result,
not an outcome comparison.

\subsubsection{Why no repaired-FC training arm exists yet}
\label{sec:norepair}

The natural control is \emph{repaired} FC rather than ReAct, and we did not have it at the time of
writing. Four obstacles were met and three dissolved on inspection; the full account, with the two
negative interventions it produced, is in Appendix~\ref{app:norepair}. In summary: a vLLM parser
plugin does not reach the training loop at all, because verl's AgentLoop parses for itself; a
parser registered inside \emph{verl's} registry does reach it and lifts recoverable calls from
0/100 to 52/100 at 1.5B, but zero of those 52 name \texttt{run\_tests}, every one targeting the
function the task asks the model to write --- the same signature as Llama's failure at a 5$\times$
smaller model, which name normalisation cannot repair because no call carries code to normalise. A
role-disambiguation few-shot aimed at that confusion made the model \emph{more fluent at emitting
the wrong call} (52 $\to$ 64 recoverable, still 0 naming the tool) and slightly worse at the task
(15 $\to$ 13).

\textbf{Correcting our own prescription.} We previously named the missing capability as guided
decoding. That was the wrong diagnosis: guided decoding \emph{forces} a format, whereas the repair
this paper argues for everywhere else is the opposite --- accept what the model already emits.
What a repaired-FC arm needs is a parser in verl's own registry accepting the bare-JSON call
Qwen2.5-Coder emits, and we have since demonstrated that code can be installed there and intercept
every generation. \textbf{What actually blocks the arm is scale.} At 1.5B a repaired parser would
have nothing to accept, and the arm would be void for the same reason we reject the Granite arm;
the checkpoint that does emit well-formed calls is 7B, and 7B full-parameter RL exhausted memory
on our single 80\,GB card. The control is a 7B experiment under parameter-efficient tuning, not a
stack-replacement project, and we scope it that way in \S\ref{sec:future}. Accordingly ReAct serves
as a positive-control interaction channel, not as a parser-repair control, and that comparison
changes protocol and interface together.

\subsubsection{Opening the channel does not, by itself, unlock multi-turn learning}
\label{sec:sufficiency}

The censoring result says a broken interface removes the tool-using branch from the experience
distribution. It does \emph{not} say that restoring the channel is \emph{sufficient} for the
policy to learn multi-turn repair. We tested that with the one configuration that delivers tool
feedback at this scale: ReAct, 75 GRPO steps, same model, data, hyper-parameters, seed and
evaluation hook as the three historical runs, executing 23{,}676 tool calls. Rescues by turn
$\geq$2 over steps 0/15/30/45/60 were 8, 6, 6, 8, 6 --- net change zero, and inside the range of
the zero-execution runs, whose rescue sequences are $[7,9,6,6,7,6,10,8,9,8,6]$,
$[8,7,6,7,7,7,7,5,6,6,8]$ and $[7,5,7,5,7,8,9,9,8,9]$. Whether tool feedback arrived made no
measurable difference on this metric. Composition barely moved either: adjacent checkpoints have
solved-set Jaccard 0.974--0.983, while 85 of 542 first-turn completions changed verbatim between
steps 15 and 30 --- the policy moves, the solved set does not (Appendix~\ref{sec:composition}).

It does not license ``interface repair unlocks multi-turn capability''; we tested the closest
available version of that claim and it failed. It licenses the narrower statement we defend:
\textbf{the interface determines whether tool-mediated trajectories exist at all, and their
absence is sufficient to explain why no multi-turn signal was available --- but their presence is
not sufficient to produce multi-turn learning at this scale and budget.} \textbf{This is a ReAct
arm, not a repaired-FC arm}: it changes protocol and interface together, so it cannot separate ``a
working channel is insufficient'' from ``ReAct in particular is insufficient''. It is 75 steps on
one seed at 1.5B against 150-step historical baselines, and the step-75 evaluation was lost when
the checkpoint write exhausted the disk (4.86\,GB required, 4.6\,GB free) after training had
completed. The trend over 0--60 is flat enough that the missing point would not change the
reading, but we note the loss rather than presenting an unexplained gap. The experiment that would
separate the two readings is named in \S\ref{sec:future}.
