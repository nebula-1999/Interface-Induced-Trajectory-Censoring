Mistral is the one family whose failure announces itself (HTTP 400 on a repeated
\texttt{{[}TOOL\_CALLS{]}} marker). The other three --- and a missing \texttt{-\/-enable-auto-tool-choice} aside ---
return HTTP 200 with \texttt{tool\_calls:\ {[}{]}}, which is byte-identical to a model that declined to
call the tool. \textbf{The claim is not that all failures are silent; it is that the silent ones
dominate and are the hardest to attribute.}

\textbf{A false-positive capability check.} The natural pre-flight test --- does
\texttt{apply\_chat\_template(tools=\ldots{})} render the schema into the prompt? --- correctly rejects
DeepSeek-Coder but \textbf{passes Qwen2.5-Coder}, whose \texttt{tokenizer\_config.json} inherits Hermes
scaffolding from Qwen2.5-Instruct while the checkpoint was never trained on it.
\textbf{Protocol support is a per-checkpoint property and is not inferable from the chat
template}: templates are inherited, training is not.

\paragraph{The residue is not the same failure in the two ladders.}
It would be wrong to read Instruct's remaining unparsed items as the same quantity as

malformed; payload well-formed and rejected only because \texttt{json.loads} does not tolerate
trailing text in the capture; or a genuine parser loss, meaning the replay succeeds where
the server did not.

Two things follow, and the second qualifies our own wording. First, the two ladders fail at
different layers: Coder emits a well-formed bare call and never the envelope, so
\texttt{hermes} not seeing it is \emph{correct behaviour}; Instruct emits the envelope and then
breaks the JSON, almost always by embedding Python whose docstring quotes or raw newlines
are left unescaped inside the \texttt{code} string. Second, \textbf{in no arm --- across the whole
archive --- did the server fail to parse a call that its own extractor would have accepted.}
``Censoring'' throughout this paper therefore means \emph{the attempt never reaches the parser
in a form it is defined to accept}, not \emph{the parser discards valid calls}. The single place
where parser strictness alone costs anything is Instruct-3B, where 26 payloads are valid
JSON followed by trailing text and a lenient \texttt{raw\_decode} would recover all of them.

\paragraph{Are these merely well-formed calls carrying useless code?}
A parse-rate control invites the objection that Instruct might emit better-shaped calls around
worse contents. Answering it needs a task-success number, so we measure one on the same items
under the same criterion (Table~\ref{tab:turn1}).

The objection does not survive at any size: Instruct solves 13--42\% of the items outright on
turn~1, rising with scale, and 13--65\% by the end of the episode. It is consistently weaker
than the code-specialised Coder at the same size, which is what one expects of a general
instruction model on a coding task, and it is nowhere near the zero that ``well-formed but
empty'' would require.

\paragraph{Multi-turn rescue is bounded by how much the interface lets through.}
The multi-turn layer of this ladder was initially void --- the host lacked \texttt{pytest}, so
every test call returned \texttt{No\ module\ named\ pytest} and the model reacted to that
string instead of a test result. We re-ran all five arms with the executor working, changing
nothing else and \textbf{not touching the probe binary}, so any drift in the parse layer would
be attributable to sampling alone. There is none: the parsed column reproduces value for value
at all five sizes (1, 11, 63, 77, 88), which also confirms that repairing the executor did not
disturb the upstream measurement.

The recovered column is the informative one. \textbf{Rescues rise with the parsed column} ---
0, +3, +6, +14, +23 against 1, 11, 63, 77, 88 --- and the two are monotone together. This
supplies a control the paper previously lacked. In the Coder ladder first-turn and final
passes are \emph{identical at every size} (31/31, 37/37, 53/53, 53/53, 68/68), and it would be
natural to read that as multi-turn repair being useless at this scale or on this task. The
Instruct ladder, on the same items, the same criterion and the same probe, shows it is not:
when the interface admits tool feedback, later turns do recover items, and they recover more
of them as more feedback is admitted. Coder's flat zero is a consequence of its parsed column
being zero, not evidence about multi-turn learning.

We therefore state the repair result as a ceiling rather than an absence:
\textbf{how much multi-turn recovers is bounded by how much of the tool channel the interface
leaves open}. That is a stronger and narrower claim than ``a working channel is not
sufficient'' (\S\ref{sec:sufficiency}), and the two are consistent: opening the channel is
necessary for rescue and does not by itself guarantee learning.

Two cross-checks. First, the recovered turn-1 numbers (13, 14, 34, 38, 42) match an offline
replay of the \emph{stored} turn-1 programs run beforehand on a machine with no GPU
(14, 14, 34, 38, 42) --- identical at four of five sizes, off by one at 1.5B. That replay was
sound for the reason given above: turn~1 precedes any observation. Second, the defect was
always confined to that host; the Coder ladder ran on different hardware and records non-zero
first-turn passes at every size, so its executor demonstrably worked.

\subsection{The same mismatch, measured on a standard benchmark}
\label{sec:bfcl}

Everything above is measured with our own probe on our own task set. A reader is entitled to
ask whether the effect is a property of that instrument. We therefore replicate it on the
Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard v4, using BFCL's official data, its multi-turn
executor and its scorer at pinned Gorilla commit
\texttt{6ea57973c7a6097fd7c5915698c54c17c5b1b6c8}, and changing \emph{only the serving adapter
configuration} between two arms of the same model --- weights, benchmark cases, decoding,
seeds, executor and scorer all held fixed. We say \emph{adapter configuration}, not
\emph{parser}: arm~2 substitutes the community Qwen2.5-Coder parser \textbf{together with its
paired chat template}, so the intervention is the pair.

We ran the $2\times2$ that separates them, on the same 200 cases and seeds:

\small

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
& documented template & dedicated template \\
\midrule
\texttt{hermes} parser & 0/200 & \textbf{0/200} \\
dedicated parser & \textbf{0/200} & 196/200 \\
\bottomrule

BFCL's own scorer gives 0.00 for the three zero cells and 0.96 / 0.19 for the fourth.
\textbf{The effect cannot be apportioned because it is not additive: both main effects are
exactly zero and all of it sits in the interaction.} Inspecting the emissions shows why. Under
the documented template the model writes a bare call inside a \texttt{```json} fence; under
the dedicated template it writes the same payload, byte for byte, inside \texttt{<tools>}
tags. Each parser then behaves correctly for the envelope it is defined to accept, and a
published benchmark score of 0.00 or 0.96 is decided by whether the two agree. This is the
sharpest available statement of the paper's thesis: \textbf{no component is defective, and
repairing one side of the contract without the other buys exactly nothing.}

One deviation from stock BFCL is required and we state it plainly. BFCL's shipped handler
for local Qwen models posts to \texttt{/v1/completions} and extracts \texttt{\textless{}tool\_call\textgreater{}}
blocks with a regular expression \emph{inside the benchmark process}. On that path the serving
parser is never invoked, so the quantity under study cannot appear. We instead route BFCL's
own OpenAI function-calling handler through \texttt{/v1/chat/completions}, adding exactly two
things: an explicit \texttt{tool\_choice:\ auto}, and trace headers joining each HTTP request to
its BFCL case id. Dataset loading, tool execution and scoring remain BFCL's code, and
\texttt{bfcl\_eval}'s own files are left byte-identical --- registration happens in memory at
import time. A recording proxy sits between benchmark and server and logs every request.

Both arms serve Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct on vLLM~0.27.1 at
\texttt{max\_model\_len\ =\ 16384}, \texttt{temperature\ =\ 0}, inference seed 0, with a subset of
100 \texttt{simple\_python} and 100 \texttt{multi\_turn\_base} cases drawn at manifest seed
20260901 and \textbf{identical in both arms}. The arms differ in one respect: arm~1 uses the
documented \texttt{hermes} parser; arm~2 uses the vendored community Qwen2.5-Coder parser with
its paired chat template.

not an estimate of it.

\paragraph{What was verified, and how far it reproduces.}
Each arm passes a two-case end-to-end smoke test before its full run, and the run is rejected
unless the result count, the score-file \texttt{total\_count} and the number of distinct case ids
in the proxy log all agree at 200 with zero HTTP errors. We ran the pair twice. The
\texttt{hermes} arm is identical across runs in every column (0/200 parsed, 166 tight, 480
requests, 0 errors). The repaired arm moves by one case between runs --- 197 versus 196 first
parsed, 99 versus 98 executed --- while \textbf{success is 19 in both}. Greedy decoding does not
make vLLM bitwise deterministic across differing batch compositions, and this arm runs eight
threads through a multi-turn executor; we therefore report the intermediate funnel columns of
this arm as $\pm 1$ rather than exact.

\subsection{A 23\% failure that survived three controls and fell to the fourth}
\label{sec:llama}

Llama-3.1-8B under FC issues a tool call on 97/100 items, but on 23 of them the call names
\textbf{the function the task asks it to implement} --- \texttt{can\_form\_word}, \texttt{check\_password\_strength},
\texttt{floyd\_warshall} --- with that function's own parameters as arguments
(\texttt{\{"tiles":\ "aabbcc",\ "word":\ "abc"\}}). A re-run restricted to those 23 items under matched
configuration reproduced 23/23 as wrong-tool calls.

\emph{This also corrects a measurement of our own}: because the probe did not originally verify
the tool name, these 23 were counted as initiations. \textbf{Llama's true \texttt{run\_tests} initiation
rate is 74/100, not 97/100} (\texttt{ERRATA.md} \S\ref{sec:related}).

\textbf{Where the residual gap lives.} With \texttt{strict} enabled, FC and ReAct have \emph{identical}
execution mechanics --- 98 calls, 0 wrong-tool, 97 executed on both sides --- yet turn-1 pass
differs 46 vs 61. Once the interface is equalised, \textbf{what remains is first-draft code
quality under the protocol, not tool-use mechanics.}

\emph{(One \texttt{strict} call is issued and syntactically valid but yields no test result: item 53
imports \texttt{seaborn}, unavailable in the sandbox, so the harness collected zero tests. It is
counted as a failure, not silently dropped.)}

The rich schema explicitly warned \emph{``this tool is not the function you are asked to
implement; do not pass the task function's parameters''} and supplied an example --- the
confusion persisted at 22/100. The CoT control is the more interesting negative: asking the
model to reason about the task's signature and edge cases \textbf{before} constructing the call
raised confusion from 23 to 59, because the parameters it had just reasoned about were the
ones it then supplied. \textbf{On an interface with an ambiguous role boundary, more reasoning
amplifies misuse rather than correcting it.}

The defensible statement is narrower than either ``model defect'' or ``configuration bug'':
\textbf{the model exhibits role confusion under unconstrained function calling, and the interface
constraint determines whether that confusion can be expressed as an environment action.}
We cannot show from these experiments that the underlying tendency persists once
constrained --- only that it stops reaching the action space. vLLM's \texttt{auto} mode is
unconstrained by default.

protocol gap measured on the same 300 items --- adapter $\to$ ReAct, 178/300 $\to$ 219/300,

\begin{quote}
Interface-induced censoring does not merely weaken a learning signal.
The tool-using branch receives \textbf{no direct on-policy gradient signal under the
observed rollout distribution}, and is \textbf{effectively inaccessible to policy-gradient
learning in this cold-start regime}.
\end{quote}

{\footnotesize
\begin{verbatim}
Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B emits nothing hermes can parse
  (probe replication under the training prompt: 0 parsed, 0 tight, 66 JSON fragments)

      -> Observation never returns; trajectories degenerate to single-turn
        -> RL optimises only a single-turn signal

\subsubsection{Opening the channel does not, by itself, unlock multi-turn learning}
\label{sec:sufficiency}

The censoring result above says a broken interface removes the tool-using branch from the
experience distribution. It does \emph{not} say that restoring the channel is \emph{sufficient}
for the policy to learn multi-turn repair. We tested that directly.

\textbf{What this arm is, and what it is not.} Since no executable repaired-FC condition exists
in this stack (previous subsection), we ran the one configuration that does deliver tool
feedback at this scale: \textbf{ReAct}, 75 GRPO steps, same model, data, hyper-parameters,
seed and evaluation hook as the three historical runs. The run executed \textbf{23{,}676 tool
calls}. \textbf{This is a ReAct arm, not a repaired-FC arm}: it changes protocol and
interface together, so it is a test of whether \emph{any} working tool channel suffices, not
an isolation of the parser. We state this explicitly because the weaker reading --- that a
parser repair was applied to the FC arm --- is not what was run and is not what we claim.

\subsection{Pressure on a broken interface makes things worse}
\label{sec:pressure}

Two independent controls point the same way. Adding a reasoning scaffold to FC raised
role confusion from 23 to \textbf{59} (\S\ref{sec:llama}): asked to analyse the task function's signature
before acting, the model supplied exactly those parameters to the tool. Replacing the
optional tool instruction with a mandatory one (\emph{``you must call \texttt{run\_tests}; do not answer
directly''}) on Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B moved parser-accepted calls not at all --- still 0/100 ---
while final pass fell from \textbf{31 to 15} and unparsable outputs rose from \textasciitilde0 to 46/100.

Neither intervention touches the interface, and both make the observable outcome worse.
Coercion cannot substitute for a working channel: the model is pushed away from the
answer it could give and toward a call that will be discarded anyway.

\textbf{Practical reading.} When an agent appears reluctant to use tools, strengthening the
instruction is the cheapest thing to try and the most likely to mislead --- it can degrade
task performance while leaving the tool-call count at zero, which looks like a model that
is both unwilling \emph{and} incapable.

\textbf{Inverted noise structure.} ReAct's turn-1 varies widely (43--56, sd 6.8) while its
final converges; FC's turn-1 is nearly constant (44--45, sd 0.58) while its final disperses.
The mechanism is compensation in the rescue count --- 19 / 16 / \textbf{29}, largest where
turn-1 was worst --- and in all three seeds the count of ``passed at turn 1 but failed
finally'' is \textbf{0}: the repair loop is monotone. \textbf{The three identical finals are a
coincidence of totals, not identical item sets}, and sd = 0.00 must not be read as ``ReAct is
perfectly stable''; \S\ref{sec:composition} gives the item-level evidence.

\begin{center}\rule{0.5\linewidth}{0.5pt}\end{center}

\subsection{Multi-turn repair stabilises the total while its composition keeps moving}
\label{sec:composition}

Two independent observations, from different axes, show the same structure.

\textbf{Across sampling seeds} (\S\ref{sec:variance}): ReAct's final pass is 72 / 72 / 72 at \texttt{temperature=0.6},
yet the sets of solved items have pairwise Jaccard 0.71--0.80 --- 15--24 items differ between
seeds. Turn-1 pass varies widely (43--56, sd 6.8) and the rescue count compensates inversely
(19 / 16 / 29).

\textbf{Across training steps} (\S\ref{sec:sufficiency}): between step 15 and step 30 of the ReAct run, 85 of 542
first-turn completions changed verbatim and 6 items flipped outcome --- 3 gained, 3 lost ---
leaving turn-1 and final pass numerically identical (340 / 346 at both checkpoints). The
policy is demonstrably moving; the aggregate is not.

The common structure is that \textbf{the repair loop absorbs perturbation upstream of it}.
Whatever the first draft does --- vary with sampling, drift with training --- items that fail
and are repairable get repaired, and the total lands in the same place. This has two
consequences worth separating:

\emph{For measurement.} An aggregate pass rate is a \textbf{poor instrument for detecting change in a
multi-turn agent}: it is stabilised by the very mechanism under study. Item-level set
comparison (Jaccard, flip counts) detects movement that the total conceals. We report both
throughout, and note that had we reported only totals we would have concluded --- twice, on
two different axes --- that nothing was happening.

\emph{For deployment.} Stability of the total under sampling noise is a property practitioners
actually want, and it is contributed by the multi-turn loop rather than by the base policy.
It is invisible in single-turn evaluation.

\textbf{Bound.} Both observations are on one model family and one task. The identical totals are
in part coincidence --- three seeds landing on exactly 72, two checkpoints landing on exactly
340/346 --- and we do not claim the aggregate is invariant, only that it is far less sensitive
than its components.
